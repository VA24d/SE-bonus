# Microservice Topologies: Choreography vs. Orchestration

Microservice architectures differ radically in how services interact.  In an **orchestrated** topology, a central coordinator invokes downstream services in sequence, creating a linear, blocking request chain.  In contrast, **choreographed** (event-driven) designs use asynchronous, parallel invocation: services react independently to events without waiting for a central controller.  Empirical studies consistently find that choreography yields lower end-to-end latency and CPU utilization than strict orchestration.  For example, a benchmark comparing a choreographed (parallel) fan-out to an orchestrated (sequential) pipeline found the choreography pattern achieved substantially higher throughput and lower CPU usage【3†L39-L44】.  These performance gains translate to energy savings: in one case, a chain of synchronous calls (“poorly bounded” microservices) consumed roughly 30–40% more CPU time and energy than an equivalent asynchronous design【52†L94-L100】.  

Quantitative measurements reinforce this: Ristova *et al.* evaluated six canonical service-dependency topologies under identical workloads, measuring CPU energy usage in kJ. They observed that *parallel* fan-out and probabilistic tree structures (both highly asynchronous) were the most energy-efficient, while *chain* and *mesh* (synchronous, sequential) designs consumed the most energy【8†L117-L122】.  For example, at larger scales the dense mesh and linear chain topologies reached nearly 39 kJ, whereas the parallel fan-out remained near the lower end of the range【8†L117-L122】.  In summary, orchestrated call chains tend to incur idle CPU and coordination overhead (wasting energy) whereas event-driven choreography leverages concurrency to improve resource utilization.  These findings indicate that choosing asynchronous, parallel interaction patterns generally reduces the Software Carbon Intensity of a microservice system, provided that any extra network traffic is carefully managed.

# Broker Efficiency: Apache Kafka vs RabbitMQ

Message brokers exhibit different energy and resource profiles based on their architecture. RabbitMQ is a traditional in-memory queue with flexible routing, while Kafka is a distributed, append-only log relying on disk I/O and batching. Benchmark studies show that Kafka achieves much higher throughput than RabbitMQ, but often with higher baseline resource use.  For example, synthesized results report Kafka sustaining on the order of 76,000–100,000+ messages per second (1 KB messages) on modest hardware, compared to only 7,000–18,000 msg/s for RabbitMQ under optimal settings【59†L897-L906】.  In practical terms, Kafka can easily outperform RabbitMQ by a factor of 4–10× in throughput, especially for small messages【59†L897-L905】【59†L919-L928】.  

This performance gap is mirrored in resource efficiency. RabbitMQ consumes notably less CPU at moderate message rates, but its efficiency saturates early.  In one test, RabbitMQ used about 60% of a CPU core to sustain 7,000 msg/s (≈8.6 msg per 1% CPU), whereas Kafka used 82% at 11,000 msg/s (≈134 msg per 1% CPU)【59†L1041-L1049】.  Normalized per message, Kafka’s CPU usage is far lower, thanks to batching, zero-copy transfer, and sequential I/O optimizations【59†L1041-L1049】.  Memory usage shows a similar pattern: at moderate loads RabbitMQ required only ~1.4 GB of RAM versus ~2.1 GB for Kafka【59†L1057-L1064】, but as throughput grows Kafka’s per-message memory footprint becomes smaller than RabbitMQ’s.  The underlying disk I/O is also critical: Kafka’s append-only writes yield far higher sustained I/O throughput, while RabbitMQ’s random-access queue storage degrades rapidly under load【59†L1075-L1083】.  In practice, RabbitMQ is more energy-efficient for low-volume, latency-sensitive scenarios (its Erlang VM and in-memory queues incur minimal overhead), but Kafka becomes more energy-efficient at large scale.  Once Kafka’s cluster is warmed up, its high throughput means less energy per message. 

To summarize, benchmarks consistently show Kafka demanding more CPU, memory, and disk resources under load, but yielding far greater throughput.  RabbitMQ’s resource usage grows quickly beyond moderate loads.  In terms of energy, this implies that small deployments or bursty workloads may consume less power with RabbitMQ, whereas high-throughput streaming pipelines will favor Kafka for lower energy per transmitted message.  (In either case, idle power draw and overhead should be accounted for, since Kafka’s disk-based design typically has a higher baseline energy cost.)

# Data Serialization: JSON vs. Protobuf

The choice of data format directly affects CPU and network energy. JSON is verbose text requiring string parsing and validation, while Protocol Buffers (Protobuf) is a compact binary format with a predefined schema.  Studies quantify dramatic resource savings when moving from JSON to Protobuf.  In a recent ICSOFT 2025 study, Shatnawi *et al.* refactored two web services from JSON to Protobuf and measured the impact. They reported a **60–80% reduction in payload size**, which translated into an **80% faster response time**, **17% lower CPU utilization**, and **18% lower energy consumption**, all *with no increase in memory use*【44†L277-L285】.  This means that for each request, far less data traverses the network and less CPU time is spent parsing, yielding a significantly smaller carbon footprint.  

An independent industrial case study on an API (PetStore) confirmed these trends: Protobuf-based requests were processed in ~5.6 s each versus ~27 s with JSON, leading to about **33% lower energy use**【33†L228-L235】.  Notably, in that experiment the Protobuf version actually used more CPU percentage at any instant (about 12.8% vs. 4.1% for JSON), but finished tasks so much faster that total energy was lower.  Memory usage was also about 10% less for Protobuf【33†L228-L235】.  In essence, the “JSON tax” manifests as both extra CPU cycles for parsing and extra networking energy for larger payloads.  By contrast, Protobuf’s binary schema enables faster serialization/deserialization and smaller wire size. The cumulative effect is a clear energy win for Protobuf (and similar binary formats) in high-throughput microservice environments【44†L277-L285】【33†L228-L235】.

Beyond these specific figures, the literature highlights the general principle that reducing data size and CPU work reduces Software Carbon Intensity. Protobuf (and other binary formats) systematically reduce network bandwidth and parsing overhead. For example, even without formal measurements, it is well known in systems benchmarks that Protobuf or Flatbuffers typically produce payloads roughly 50–80% smaller than JSON while parsing many times faster.  These reductions in bytes and cycles directly map to less energy drawn by network switches and CPUs.

# Summary: Impact on Software Carbon Intensity (SCI)

The three pillars above all influence the system’s overall energy per workload, and thus its Software Carbon Intensity (SCI).  Architecturally, choosing asynchronous, event-driven flows minimizes idle CPU time and speeds up completion, cutting energy per request【3†L39-L44】【52†L94-L100】.  Likewise, selecting the right message broker for the load balances baseline overhead versus per-message cost: a Kafka cluster may draw more power at rest but amortizes that over millions of messages, whereas a lightweight broker like RabbitMQ may save energy on light workloads.  Finally, using compact binary formats dramatically lowers both CPU and network energy: studies find on the order of 18–33% energy savings simply by replacing JSON with Protobuf【44†L277-L285】【33†L228-L235】.  In combination, these architectural decisions shape the carbon efficiency of a microservice system. Each decision—topology, broker, serialization—affects resource utilization (CPU cycles, memory, I/O) and therefore the carbon emissions per unit of work. A system that optimizes along all three dimensions (asynchronous choreography, high-throughput broker, and compact data format) will have a substantially lower SCI than one built with synchronous chains, chatty queues, and verbose payloads. In other words, greener microservice design is achieved by minimizing unnecessary CPU blocking, data transfer, and parsing overhead, as evidenced by the studies above. Together, these findings provide a quantitative foundation for energy-aware design choices in event-driven microservice architectures, in line with the goals of the SCI specification.
